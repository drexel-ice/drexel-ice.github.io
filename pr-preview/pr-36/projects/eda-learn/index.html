EDA-Learn: Open and Reproducible Machine Learning for Electronic Design Automation

Learned models that predict expensive late-stage chip metrics from early design stages, and the open schema, dataset, and benchmarks that make machine learning for design automation reproducible.

Digital physical design runs as a sequence of interdependent stages — logic synthesis, floorplanning, placement, clock tree synthesis, routing, and signoff. Decisions taken early determine the interconnect geometry, timing, power and routability that are only measured accurately at the end, after routing and extraction. When the final numbers miss their targets, the design returns to an earlier stage and the pipeline runs again, at a cost measured in hours to days per pass.

This program works on the feedback problem that creates: estimating a downstream outcome from the information available at an earlier stage, so that corrections can be made while they are still cheap. The work began with graph neural networks for timing and parasitic prediction, generalized them into a reusable framework, and then turned to a second problem that limits the whole field — that most published results in machine learning for electronic design automation (EDA) cannot be reproduced or fairly compared, because they depend on proprietary tools, confidential designs, and incompatible data formats. Addressing that required building open infrastructure: a standardized schema, a large open dataset, and a public benchmark.

The prediction problem

At any point in the flow, a designer has source-stage information and wants a target-stage measurement that does not exist yet. Conventional estimators fill that gap with analytical proxies — wireload models, half-perimeter wirelength, Steiner trees, congestion heuristics. A learned predictor is trained to do the same job from the same early information, and both are scored against the real post-routing measurement once the flow completes.

Conventional early estimation and machine learning prediction of a downstream metric. Information available at an earlier source stage feeds both a conventional estimator and a learned predictor; each estimate is evaluated against the target-stage measurement, which is ground truth for scoring rather than an input at inference time.

Framing the problem this way matters for honesty as much as accuracy. Keeping the target-stage measurement strictly out of the inputs is what prevents downstream information from leaking into an early predictor and producing accuracy that would not survive real use.

Representing circuits for learning

A circuit is natively relational, which makes graph representations a direct fit. The work uses three, each matched to the granularity of the quantity being predicted: a netlist graph of the whole circuit, timing-path graphs extracted from it as subgraphs for per-path delay quantities, and an interconnect graph in which wire segments themselves become nodes for spatial parasitic and wirelength prediction.

A sample ISCAS'89 benchmark as (a) a netlist graph, with inputs, outputs and gates as typed nodes, and (b) the timing-path graphs extracted from it. Timing reports drive the extraction, so each subgraph isolates one signal's journey through the circuit.

Node features are drawn from the standard artifacts a flow already produces — design constraints, LEF/DEF geometry, structural attributes such as logic level and fan-out, STA timing reports, and SPEF parasitics — so nothing in the representation requires instrumenting the tools.

A generalized graph learning framework

The first prediction tasks were solved as separate pipelines. They were then unified into EDA-ML, a task-agnostic framework in which any prediction is posed the same way: observe stage X, predict metric M at stage Y, at a stated granularity. Dataset generation, graph construction, and the training and evaluation flow are shared; what changes between tasks is the graph representation, the prediction level, and the baseline the model must beat.

The generalized framework for downstream metric prediction: an automated design flow produces the stored artifacts (netlist, timing reports, extracted parasitics), which are converted into netlist, timing-path and interconnect graphs, which in turn feed a common training and evaluation flow. Arrival time prediction and interconnect parasitic prediction are two instantiations of the same recipe.

Two results followed from the framework. Posing both arrival time and parasitic prediction through one recipe showed the approach generalizes across tasks rather than being tuned per problem — reported in the EDA-ML paper. And a model trained for one technology node can be carried to a newer one by freezing its layers and fine-tuning a single appended layer on a small amount of target-node data, instead of regenerating a dataset and retraining from scratch — reported in the transfer learning paper. Both are linked under Publications, where the full results are reported.

EDA-Schema: an open foundation

Better predictors are only half the problem. A model trained on a confidential dataset produced by a commercial tool cannot be reproduced outside the group that built it, and its reported accuracy cannot be meaningfully compared against a model trained on someone else’s private data. Published accuracy figures across the field are, for this reason, largely not comparable to one another. EDA-Schema attacks that directly.

A schema for the whole design flow

EDA-Schema defines a stage-aware property-graph data model for digital circuits spanning synthesis through detailed routing. At every stage, structural netlists, timing reports, parasitic extraction, layout geometry, power delivery analysis, and quality-of-results (QoR) metrics are parsed out of the standard tool outputs and organized into one consistent representation.

The EDA-Schema-V2 entity relationship diagram. Yellow entities capture the design flow hierarchy (flow, stage, constraints); blue entities hold structural design data — netlist, clock tree, timing path, power delivery network, and their constituent gates, nets, pins and arcs; green entities record quality-of-results metrics for cell, area, power, timing and routability.

Because the schema is derived from standard interchange formats (LEF/DEF, SPEF, Liberty, STA reports), it stays compatible with commercial flows even though the reference dataset is generated entirely with open tools.

Multimodal by design

A circuit is not only a graph. EDA-Schema-V2 extends the model so that each design instance carries graphs, spatial layout images, and scalar heatmaps side by side, which lets graph networks, convolutional networks, and hybrid architectures all train from the same source of truth rather than from separately prepared datasets.

The graph modality: a circuit as a heterogeneous netlist graph, with I/O ports, gates, nets and pins as typed nodes and the logical and physical connections between them as edges.
The image modality, for the ac97_ctrl circuit on the Nangate 45 nm node: (a) combinational cell placement, (b) sequential cell placement, (c) pin locations, (d) overall routing, (e) metal 1 routing, (f) metal 5 routing. Pixel size is fixed to the minimum width of metal 1, so the maps scale consistently across technology nodes.
The heatmap modality: Rectangular Uniform wire DensitY (RUDY) routability maps — (a) net-based, (b) pin-based, (c) long-range and (d) short-range — which estimate routing demand across the layout before routing is run. The schema also stores IR-drop and electromigration heatmaps for the power delivery network.

Generated with open tools, end to end

The dataset is produced by automating the OpenROAD flow over public benchmark circuits and open process design kits, sweeping clock period, utilization, aspect ratio and other parameters. The sweeps deliberately include barely-passing and barely-failing operating points, so the data contains the marginal cases where prediction actually matters rather than only comfortable ones. Nothing in the pipeline requires a commercial license or an NDA, which is what makes the resulting data redistributable.

A parallel standards effort is pushing the same representation toward industry adoption, through both the Si2 Schema Working Group and the Si2 Logic Block Composition (LBC) Extend & Expand Working Group, so that data means the same thing across tools, vendors and labs.

A public benchmark

On top of the dataset, EDA-Schema-V2 defines twelve standardized prediction tasks, each with fixed evaluation metrics and the EDA tool’s own estimate as the reference baseline.

Prediction target Granularity
Total area, total power, total wirelength Whole circuit
Worst arrival time, worst slack, total negative slack Whole circuit
Interconnect length Per interconnect
Timing-path arrival time, timing-path slack Per timing path
Net-arc delay Per net arc
Cell-arc delay, cell-arc slew Per cell arc

Models are scored with leave-one-circuit-out evaluation, so a reported result reflects generalization to a circuit the model has never seen rather than memorization of a familiar one — a distinction that ordinary random splits obscure, because fragments of the same circuit then appear in both training and test data.

Results are published to a public leaderboard, which reports every released model against the tool baseline for each task, PDK and design stage.

Research arc

  1. 2022–2023 — Establish the approach. Graph neural networks predict post-routing gate arrival time and interconnect parasitics from earlier stages, with attention to the long tail where worst-case behavior lives.
  2. 2024 — Generalize and migrate. EDA-ML poses multiple prediction tasks through one framework, and transfer learning carries a trained model between technology nodes.
  3. 2025 — Extend and survey. A differentiable wirelength estimator is built to drop into gradient-based placement optimizers, and a survey of representation learning for EDA maps the field and names data standardization as its central bottleneck.
  4. 2024–2026 — Build the infrastructure. EDA-Schema and its multimodal successor EDA-Schema-V2 deliver an open schema, dataset and benchmark, carried toward an industry standard through the Si2 working groups.

What began as a set of better predictors became the public infrastructure needed to build and fairly compare such predictors.

Current directions. Work is ongoing on EDA-OpenML, an open-weight framework for reproducible and data-efficient neural prediction, and EDA-AutoML, which automates neural architecture and hyperparameter selection — including an LLM-assisted approach to generating model configurations from a task description. Results will be reported here as they are published.
